# Good Video Principles Skill

## Purpose
Provide high-level narrative and structural editing frameworks that keep viewers engaged throughout a video essay or tutorial, defining the exact structures for hooks, pacing resets, argument pivots, and call-to-actions.

## When To Use
- Structuring the script flow and narrative architecture of a video.
- Organizing the pacing, transitions, and timing resets in a timeline.
- Formatting the opening hook, midpoint climax/pivot, and outro pay-offs.

## Core Rules

### Rule 1: Dual-Stage Hooks (Macro Threat + High-Stakes Visual)

- Instruction: Within the first 7 seconds, frame a trend/topic as a macro-level business disruption, creative threat, or stark contradiction. Pair this narration immediately with a high-stakes visual (e.g. stock market crash charts, a guest's face, or a "before-state" edit comparison).
- Use when: Establishing the opening hook of any informational or educational video.
- Avoid when: Transitioning between late chapters or presenting deep technical specifications.

### Rule 2: Structured Pacing Resets

- Instruction: Periodically cut to a centered talking-head shot (often isolated on a clean, dark background) between dense visual-only sequences, diagrams, or spec tables to slow pacing and reset viewer focus.
- Use when: Bridging different chapters, shifting arguments, or transitioning after complex statistical charts.
- Avoid when: Building rhythmic, fast-paced lists or rapid musical montages.

### Rule 3: Midpoint Narrative Reversals

- Instruction: Pivot the logical direction of the argument at the video midpoint (e.g., transition from upside benefits to downside conflicts, general definitions to specific corporate actions, or tools listing to sociological theory).
- Use when: Structuring video timelines longer than 3 minutes to prevent retention drops.
- Avoid when: Creating ultra-short form videos (under 60 seconds).

- Use when: Executing outro chapters, concluding technical walkthroughs, or summarizing case studies.
- Avoid when: Designing intermediate chapter headings or mid-point transitions.

### Rule 5: Isolated Diagnostic CTAs

- Instruction: Separate calls-to-action from the talking head by framing them as structured step-by-step diagnostic workflows or package grids listing clear qualification criteria and timelines.
- Use when: Directing viewers to events, newsletters, masterclasses, or product pages.
- Avoid when: Running primary educational body chapters.

## Decision Checklist
1. [ ] Does the hook hook the viewer within 7 seconds using both a macro-level stake (dialogue) and a concrete asset (visual)?
2. [ ] Are talking-head resets placed after dense diagram sequences to prevent visual fatigue?
3. [ ] Does the video structure contain a logical pivot or midpoint reversal to maintain engagement?
4. [ ] Does the ending resolve with a unified visual payoff or a memorable strategic tradeoff?
5. [ ] Is the CTA stylized as a diagnostic card rather than a generic spoken reminder?

## Failure Modes
- **Weak Hook Framing**: Introducing the subject immediately without establishing high stakes or a macro threat.
- **Visual Fatigue**: Stacking multiple complex graphics, spec sheets, and diagrams consecutively without inserting a talking-head reset.
- **Vague Outros**: Ending the video with a generic "Thanks for watching" rather than a strategic tradeoff or venture prompt.

## Example Execution Pattern
1. *Opening (0-7s)*: Host says: "AI is wiping out local coding jobs." (Visual: Stock market tickers dropping, Satya Nadella tweet overlay).
2. *Body (Resets)*: Show diagrams of systems. Cut back to host on black background to summarize.
3. *Midpoint (3m)*: Pivot: "But the threat isn't the AI—it's who controls the GPU clusters."
4. *Outro (Ending)*: Show a final consolidated map of sovereign data centers. Frame the CTA inside a "Developer Bootcamp Eligibility" card.
